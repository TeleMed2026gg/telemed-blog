A cost center call center is judged by what it spends. A profit center is judged by what it spends and what it earns. The difference usually isn't the people or the software. It's whether finance and operations agree on how to measure the revenue the team protects and creates, and whether agents have the authority to act on it.

Most contact centers already influence revenue. They just don't get credit for it, so they get managed like an expense.

## Why contact centers get treated as cost centers

Cost is easy to see. Payroll, licenses and vendor invoices all land on the P&L each month under one owner. Revenue influence is scattered. A saved customer shows up as renewal revenue in another department, and a recovered sale shows up under sales.

When only the expense is visible, the logical move is to shrink it. Teams cut handle time, trim staff and push customers into self-service. Each step looks efficient on paper. The lost revenue happens somewhere nobody is looking.

That's a poor trade given what's at stake:

- Bain's Fred Reichheld and Christine Detrick [wrote](https://media.bain.com/Images/MM_Loyalty_prescription_cutting_costs.pdf) that in financial services, a 5% increase in customer retention produced more than a 25% increase in profit. The exact figure won't carry over to every industry, but the direction is the point.
- [Forrester's 2024 US CX Index](https://www.forrester.com/press-newsroom/forrester-2024-us-customer-experience-index/?) reports that customer-obsessed companies achieve 41% faster revenue growth, 49% faster profit growth and 51% higher customer retention than those that aren't. Only 3% of companies qualified. That's a correlation, not proof the contact center caused the growth, but it shows where the value sits.
- In a [Gartner survey](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-20-gartner-survey-finds-73-percent-of-csos-are-prioritizing-growth-from-existing-customers-for-2025) of 243 sales leaders, 73% said they were prioritizing growth from existing customers for 2025. Your contact center talks to those customers every day.

AI won't resolve this by itself. Gartner [found in late 2025](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-02-03-gartner-predicts-half-of-companies-that-cut-customer-service-staff-due-to-ai-will-rehire-by-2027) that only 20% of customer service leaders had reduced agent staffing because of AI, and it predicts half of the companies that attributed cuts to AI will rehire for similar work by 2027. Automation can take on routine volume. Conversations that keep or win a customer still need judgment.

## Cost center vs. profit center: what actually changes

| Dimension | Cost center | Profit center |
|---|---|---|
| Executive question | How do we spend less? | What do we earn per dollar spent? |
| Main number | Cost per contact | Revenue impact minus fully loaded cost |
| Handle time | Reduce it everywhere | Reduce it where it adds no value |
| Agent authority | Follow the script | Retain, rebook, offer or escalate |
| Data | Telephony reports | Telephony joined with CRM and finance data |
| Budget review | Annual cuts | Investment case by program |

The title doesn't matter. Some companies keep the contact center on a cost-center budget and still report a revenue contribution next to it. For most businesses, that's the more practical first step, because it doesn't require reorganizing finance.

## Build a simple contact center P&L

You don't need a complex attribution model. You need a one-page view that finance helps design and can check.

1. **Pick the outcomes.** Choose two or three that are closest to money, such as saved accounts, converted leads, recovered payments or rebooked appointments.
2. **Assign a conservative value to each.** Use finance's numbers: average order value, gross margin or first-year contract value. Use margin rather than revenue if that's what your CFO prefers.
3. **Set a baseline.** Pull 60 to 90 days of data before any change. Use a control group if volume allows it.
4. **Agree on attribution rules.** Define what counts, how long after the contact it counts, and who gets credit when two teams touched the customer.
5. **Load the costs fully.** Include salaries, benefits, supervision, technology, vendor fees and training.
6. **Audit a sample.** Review a random set of attributed outcomes every month, and report what failed.

Here's a hypothetical example, using made-up numbers to show the math. A subscription business handles cancellation calls and adds a trained retention group.

| Line | Monthly |
|---|---|
| Cancellation requests handled | 1,000 |
| Save rate at baseline | 10% |
| Save rate after the change | 16% |
| Additional saved accounts | 60 |
| Annual gross margin per saved account | $300 |
| Annual margin protected by this month's saves | $18,000 |
| Added monthly cost of the program | $8,000 |
| Net contribution from one month of saves | $10,000 |

That table tells a CFO more than a cost-per-contact trend. It also exposes the assumptions, so people can challenge them. A real version should adjust for saved customers who leave a few months later.

## Where a contact center can earn its keep

The opportunities vary by industry, but they tend to fall into four groups:

- **Protect:** retention saves, complaint recovery and proactive outreach before renewal.
- **Convert:** faster lead response, better handling of inbound sales calls and abandoned-cart follow-up.
- **Recover:** payment reminders, past-due accounts and missed appointments.
- **Expand:** relevant add-ons offered after the issue is solved.

Healthcare offers a clear example. In our experience, calling patients who missed appointments and rescheduling them recovers up to about 40% of those visits. One cardiovascular client increased monthly billing by several thousand dollars without adding hours, simply by running its scheduling line better. Nothing about that needed a new product, only a team measured on filled appointments. ## What to change in operations

Measurement alone won't move the numbers. Three operating changes usually have to follow.

- **Give agents authority.** Set clear limits on credits, offers, payment plans and rebooking, and let agents use them without escalation.
- **Change the scorecard.** Keep service level, quality and complaint rate as guardrails. Add one revenue outcome per program. Don't tie pay to sales alone, or you'll train agents to push.
- **Route the work.** Send retention and high-value contacts to your strongest people. Let automation handle confirmations, status checks and simple requests.

If you outsource, contract the same way. A vendor paid only by the hour has little reason to improve yield per hour. We cover that shift in [how to turn an outsourcing partner into a revenue engine](/blog/outsourcing-as-revenue-partner/).

## When a cost center is the right model

Don't force a revenue story onto every queue. A cost model fits when:

- Contacts are purely transactional, such as password resets or address changes.
- You can't connect outcomes to customer or financial data.
- Volume is too low to show a real difference from the baseline.
- The product has a defect, and the right goal is fewer contacts altogether.

In those cases, reduce the contact volume and cost honestly. Claiming revenue you can't verify will cost you credibility with finance, and that credibility is what the whole approach depends on.

## Key takeaways

- A cost center reports what it spends. A profit center also reports what it earns.
- You can start without reorganizing: add a revenue contribution view next to the existing budget.
- Build the P&L with finance, using conservative values, a baseline and monthly audits.
- Agent authority, scorecards and routing have to change along with the math.
- Keep a cost model for work with no measurable link to revenue.
