Outsourcing ROI should measure what a partner earns for you, not just what it saves. Cost per hour tells you the price of an input. A useful ROI model adds the revenue a partner protects or creates, such as retained customers, converted leads and recovered payments, and subtracts the full cost of the engagement, including transition and management time. Finance and operations should agree on that model before the contract starts.

## Why cost per hour is the wrong yardstick

Cost per hour is easy to compare across proposals, which is why procurement teams use it. But it says nothing about output. Two teams at the same rate can close very different numbers of sales, keep very different numbers of customers and collect very different amounts of cash.

Executives seem to know that savings alone don't hold up. In [Deloitte's 2024 Global Outsourcing Survey](https://www.deloitte.com/global/en/issues/work/global-outsourcing-survey.html), 80% of executives planned to maintain or increase investment in third-party outsourcing. Yet only 25% said they were seeing reductions in vendor service costs or improvements in service quality, and 70% had brought some previously outsourced work back in-house in the past five years.

Priorities had already started to shift. In Deloitte's [2022 survey](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-shared/docs/collections/2023/gx-global-outsourcing-survey.pdf), 57% of executives named cost reduction as the primary driver of traditional outsourcing, down from 70% two years earlier.

Gartner made a similar point about pricing. In 2021 it [predicted](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2021-05-25-gartner-predicts-by-2025-60-percent-of-finance-and-accounting-outsourcing-contracts-will-not-be-renewed) that 60% of finance and accounting organizations would not renew existing BPO contracts by 2025, citing outdated pricing models that don't drive process improvement. Pricing by headcount rewards adding people, not getting results.

## The four parts of a complete outsourcing ROI model

A useful model has four parts. Each needs a number your CFO accepts.

| Component | What it captures | Example measures |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Fully loaded cost of the engagement versus doing it in-house | Vendor fees, internal oversight time, tools, transition costs |
| Revenue | Money the partner creates or protects | Leads converted, payments collected, appointments recovered, upsells accepted |
| Retention | Customers kept who would have left | Saves on cancellation calls, renewal rate, repeat purchase rate |
| Risk and capacity | Losses avoided and growth enabled | Coverage during turnover, after-hours capacity, compliance errors avoided |

Most ROI models stop after the first row. The other three are where a good partner earns its fee.

## Why retention belongs in the model

Retention is often the largest number in the model and the one most often left out. As [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2014/10/the-value-of-keeping-the-right-customers) summarized from Bain research, increasing customer retention rates by 5% increases profits by 25% to 95%, and acquiring a new customer costs five to 25 times more than keeping an existing one. Your numbers will differ, but the direction holds in most industries.

Customer experience also affects cost. [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/best-of-both-worlds-customer-experience-for-more-revenues-and-lower-costs) found that companies that improved the customer journey could raise revenue by 10% to 15% while lowering cost to serve by 15% to 20%. A partner that resolves issues on the first contact reduces repeat calls and keeps customers at the same time.

## How to build the model in five steps

1. **Pick two or three revenue-linked outcomes.** Choose the ones closest to cash: converted leads, collected payments, saved accounts or rebooked appointments.
2. **Assign a conservative value to each.** Use finance's numbers, such as average order value, gross margin or first-year contract value.
3. **Set a baseline before the start.** Pull 60 to 90 days of history. Use a control group if volume allows it.
4. **Agree on attribution rules in writing.** Define what counts, the time window and how credit is shared when two teams touch a customer.
5. **Review monthly with finance.** Compare outcomes with baseline and fully loaded cost, and audit a sample of attributed results.

## Example: two ways to score the same partner

Consider an outsourced team that handles inbound customer calls and follows up on missed or abandoned contacts.

- **Cost-only view:** the team costs less per hour than an in-house team. ROI equals the hourly difference times hours worked.
- **Complete view:** the same team also calls back missed contacts, reschedules cancellations and saves some customers who call to cancel. Each of those outcomes has a value finance already uses.

In healthcare, where we operate, this is concrete. In our experience, calling and rescheduling patients who missed appointments recovers up to about 40% of those visits, and systematically returning missed calls recovers up to about 10% more appointments. Those results rarely show up in a cost-per-hour comparison, but they show up in revenue. We describe this approach in more detail in [stop buying seats](/blog/outsourcing-as-revenue-partner/).

## Don't ignore the people side

Automation changes the math, but it doesn't remove the need for skilled people. In a [Gartner survey released in 2026](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260428850485/en/), 85% of customer service and support leaders said they were expanding human agent responsibilities, and only 31% had implemented or planned AI-related frontline layoffs. Score your partner on how well its people and its tools work together, not on how many seats it can cut.

## When outsourcing ROI is hard to prove

Some work doesn't have a clean link to revenue, such as internal IT tickets or back-office data entry. For those, cost, quality and turnaround time may be the right measures. Don't force a revenue story onto work that doesn't have one. Your CFO will notice.

ROI is also hard to prove without a baseline. If you can't measure the current state, spend the first month measuring it before you change anything.

## Key takeaways

- Cost per hour measures price, not value. A complete ROI model adds revenue, retention and risk.
- Most executives keep investing in outsourcing, but few report lower costs or better quality, so measure outcomes.
- Retention and first-contact resolution often outweigh hourly savings.
- Set a baseline, agree on attribution rules with finance and review results monthly.
- For work with no clear revenue link, measure cost, quality and speed instead.
