Insurance eligibility verification services confirm a patient's coverage and benefits before the visit, so problems get fixed before a claim goes out. A good process checks more than active coverage: it also confirms plan rules, patient responsibility, referrals and authorizations. Done on a fixed schedule, it's one of the cheapest denial prevention steps a practice has.

## Why eligibility verification matters more now

Denials remain high. In Experian Health's [2026 State of Claims survey](https://www.experianplc.com/newsroom/press-releases/2026/experian-health-s-annual-state-of-claims-survey-finds-denials-re) of 204 healthcare professionals, 40% said 10% or more of their claims are denied, and 65% said submitting clean claims has become harder over the past 12 months.

Two findings from that survey, [reported by Medical Economics](https://www.medicaleconomics.com/view/why-are-claims-being-denied-in-2026-services-not-covered-now-tops-the-list), point straight at the front end:

- 53% named "services not covered" among their top three denial reasons. It ranked first for the first time since the survey began in 2022.
- 43% said inaccurate data collected at registration and intake causes 5% to 15% or more of their denials.

The first finding matters most. Confirming a patient is active doesn't tell you a service is covered. If your team stops at "active," you're leaving the top denial reason unchecked.

The results weren't broken out by organization type, so they aren't specific to physician practices. Compare them with your own denial report.

## What to verify before every visit

Use the same checklist each time. Record what you found, who found it and when.

| Check | What to confirm | Denial or problem it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| Patient identity | Name, date of birth and member ID match the payer's record | Patient not found, member ID errors |
| Active coverage | Coverage is active on the date of service | Coverage terminated or not yet effective |
| Plan and network | Plan type and provider's network status | Out-of-network surprises |
| Covered benefit | The planned service is covered, with any limits | Service not covered, visit limits reached |
| Patient responsibility | Copay, deductible remaining, coinsurance | Unpaid balances, patient complaints |
| Referral and authorization | Whether the service needs either one | Missing authorization or referral |
| Coordination of benefits | Which plan is primary | Wrong payer billed |

For more on the authorization step, see our guide to [prior authorization outsourcing](/blog/prior-authorization-outsourcing-2026/).

## When to run the check

A single check doesn't hold up, because coverage can change between booking and the visit. This schedule works for most outpatient practices:

1. **At scheduling.** Capture a clean member ID and payer. Ask if anything changed.
2. **One to three business days before the visit.** Run the full benefit check. Call the patient if something is wrong, while there's still time to fix it.
3. **At check-in.** Reconfirm for higher-risk patients, such as those with Medicaid, recent job changes or first visits of the year.
4. **Before high-cost services.** Confirm benefits and authorization again for imaging, procedures and infusions.

January deserves extra staffing. Plans renew, deductibles reset and many patients have new cards they forgot to mention.

## Medicaid changes make 2027 harder

Starting in 2027, states must [recheck eligibility every six months](https://www.hfma.org/payment-reimbursement-and-managed-care/medicaid-six-month-eligibility-redeterminations-cms-guidance/) for most adults in the Medicaid expansion population, instead of once a year. Many of those adults will also face work requirements.

[Physicians Practice](https://www.physicianspractice.com/view/medicaid-work-requirements-will-land-on-the-front-desk-starting-jan-1) lays out the front desk impact. Work requirements begin January 1, 2027, and states can seek a delay, so check your state's timeline. A patient may be covered on the date of the visit and disenrolled before the claim is processed, and some patients who lose coverage will become self-pay.

If you see expansion patients, plan on these changes:

- Run the check at the visit, not only at scheduling.
- Train the front desk to explain a coverage gap and direct patients back to their state agency.
- Set a clear self-pay policy before January.

## Manual vs. electronic verification

Most routine checks should be electronic. The [2024 CAQH Index](https://www.caqh.org/hubfs/Index/2024%20Index%20Report/CAQH_IndexReport_2024_FINAL.pdf), using 2023 data, put provider labor cost per eligibility transaction at these levels:

| Method | Provider labor cost per transaction |
|---|---|
| Manual | $8.57 |
| Partially electronic | $4.46 |
| Electronic | $2.00 |

CAQH also estimated 12 minutes of savings per transaction when moving from manual or partial methods to electronic.

Electronic checks still have limits. They may not show everything you need for a specialty service, and the response can be vague. [Experian Health reports](https://www.experian.com/blogs/healthcare/common-causes-of-eligibility-related-claim-denials/) that 55% of providers say each eligibility re-check takes at least 10 minutes. That's why the target isn't zero human work. It's automation for routine checks and trained people for the exceptions.

A practical split:

- **Automated:** batch eligibility runs for tomorrow's schedule and the next two days.
- **Human:** rejected responses, new patients, secondary coverage, high-cost services and anything the automated check can't confirm.

## When eligibility verification services make sense

Consider outsourcing eligibility verification when:

- Front desk staff answer phones and check in patients while also calling payers.
- Checks often slip to the morning of the visit, or don't happen.
- Your denial report shows eligibility, registration or coverage issues month after month.
- You see complex plans, high-cost services or several payers with changing rules.
- Your schedule swings so much that staffing for peak volume isn't practical.

Keep it in-house when your volume is low, your payer mix is simple, your automation handles nearly everything and your denial data is clean. Paying a vendor won't help if the true problem is a bad intake form or registration errors no one has corrected. Fix those first.

Don't forget the patient side. A verified balance only helps if someone explains it to the patient before the visit, in their language.

## What to measure

Before changing anything, pull a 90-day baseline. Then review these monthly:

- Share of scheduled visits verified at least one business day ahead
- Denials by reason: eligibility, registration, not covered, authorization
- Exceptions found before the visit and resolved
- Same-day reschedules caused by coverage problems
- Patient balances collected at or before the visit

Track eligibility-related denials next to your wider [denial management work](/blog/denial-management-services/). Fixing them up front costs less than appealing them later.

## Key takeaways

- Verifying active coverage isn't enough. Check the benefit, patient responsibility, referrals and authorizations.
- "Services not covered" is now the top denial reason named in Experian Health's survey.
- Run checks at scheduling, one to three days before the visit, and at check-in for higher-risk patients.
- Medicaid's six-month redeterminations and work requirements will make coverage less stable starting in 2027.
- Automate routine checks, and keep trained people on the exceptions.
