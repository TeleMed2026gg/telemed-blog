Revenue cycle management (RCM) for small practices is worth outsourcing when billing depends on one or two people, denials and aging A/R are growing, or the owner is doing claims work after clinic hours. It's not worth it when an experienced in-house team already keeps denials low and A/R current. The right answer comes from a few numbers you can pull this week, not from a vendor pitch.

## Why small practices feel billing pressure first

Small groups are still a large share of US medicine, but they're shrinking. The [AMA Physician Practice Benchmark Survey](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/private-practices/smaller-share-doctors-private-practice-ever) found that 42.2% of physicians were in private practice in 2024, down from 60.1% in 2012. The share working in practices of 10 or fewer physicians fell from 61.4% to 47.4% over the same period.

Part of the reason is that the back office keeps getting heavier while payment rates don't keep pace:

- **Payment rates.** CMS's [CY 2027 Physician Fee Schedule final rule](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/calendar-year-cy-2027-medicare-physician-fee-schedule-final-rule) sets the conversion factor for most physicians at $32.63, a 2.31% decrease from 2026.
- **Denials.** [KFF's analysis](https://www.kff.org/private-insurance/claims-denials-and-appeals-in-aca-marketplace-plans-in-2023/) of ACA Marketplace plans found insurers denied about 20% of in-network claims in 2023, and fewer than 1% of denied claims were appealed.
- **Collection speed.** In a July 2026 [MGMA Stat poll](https://www.mgma.com/mgma-stat/days-in-a/r-holds-steady-for-most-practices-but-payer-pressure-persists-in-2026), 32% of practices said their days in A/R were higher than a year earlier. Only 22% said they were lower.

A large health system absorbs this with a billing department, a denials team and analysts. A five-provider practice absorbs it with one biller, a front desk and the owner's evenings.

## Signs that outsourcing RCM makes sense

Look for patterns, not one bad month. Outsourcing is usually worth evaluating when two or more of these are true:

1. **Single point of failure.** One biller holds the payer logins, the workarounds and the knowledge of which claims are stuck. A vacation or resignation stops cash.
2. **A/R over 90 days keeps growing.** Old claims get harder to collect each week. If no one has time to work them, they become write-offs.
3. **Denials get reworked, not prevented.** The same denial reasons repeat because no one has time to trace them back to registration, eligibility or coding.
4. **The owner does billing.** Every hour a physician or practice owner spends on claims is an hour not seeing patients or running the business.
5. **You can't answer basic questions.** If you don't know your denial rate, days in A/R or collection rate by payer, you're managing blind.
6. **Hiring is slow.** The [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/medical-records-and-health-information-technicians.htm) projects 8% growth for medical records specialists, the category that includes billing and coding staff, from 2025 to 2035, which it calls much faster than average. Experienced people have options.

## When to keep RCM in-house

Outsourcing isn't always the right call. Keep billing in-house, or outsource only one piece, when:

- An experienced biller is in place, denials are low, A/R is current and you have backup coverage.
- Your volume is very low and a part-time in-house person handles it without backlog.
- Your specialty relies on a payer mix or local contract knowledge that lives with one long-tenured employee, and that person isn't leaving.
- You're about to change your EHR or practice management system. Stabilize the new system first, then evaluate.

A partial handoff often works well here. Many practices keep charge entry and coding in-house and outsource eligibility checks, payment posting or aged A/R. We cover the cost side of that decision in [the real cost of an in-house medical biller](/blog/cost-of-in-house-medical-biller/).

## Full RCM vs. partial outsourcing: a comparison

| Model | What the partner does | Best fit | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full RCM | Eligibility, charge entry, coding, submission, posting, denials, A/R | Practices with no stable billing team | Losing visibility if reporting is weak |
| Back-end only | Payment posting, denials, A/R follow-up | Practices with good front-end data but slow collections | Root causes at registration stay unfixed |
| Front-end only | Eligibility, benefits, prior authorizations | Practices with many eligibility or authorization denials | Coding and follow-up gaps remain |
| Project-based | Cleanup of A/R over 90 days | Backlog after staff turnover | Backlog returns if the process doesn't change |

## How to evaluate an RCM partner

Price matters, but it's the last question, not the first. Ask these before you compare fees:

- **Where does the work happen?** The partner should work inside your practice management system and clearinghouse, with logins you control and can revoke.
- **Who does the work?** Ask whether the team is dedicated to your practice or shared across many clients. Dedicated teams learn your payers and providers faster.
- **What do you report, and how often?** At minimum: denial rate and top denial reasons, days in A/R, A/R over 90 days, and collections compared with expected reimbursement.
- **How do you handle PHI?** A partner that touches patient data is a business associate under HIPAA. We sign a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with healthcare clients. Ask how access is limited and logged. Our article on [whether offshore medical billing is HIPAA compliant](/blog/is-offshore-medical-billing-hipaa-compliant/) covers the questions to ask.
- **What happens if you leave?** Your data, payer setups and work queues should stay in your systems.
- **Can you start small?** A defined first scope, such as aged A/R or eligibility, lets you measure results before you hand off more.

## What the numbers should look like after 90 days

Set a baseline before the handoff: denial rate, days in A/R, A/R over 90 days and monthly collections. Then review the same numbers at 30, 60 and 90 days.

[MGMA](https://www.mgma.com/mgma-stat/days-in-a/r-holds-steady-for-most-practices-but-payer-pressure-persists-in-2026) reports that first-submission denial rates have held around 7% to 8% over the past four years, and notes that practices can get below 5%. Use your own baseline and payer mix to set targets, not someone else's average.

In our experience, the first 30 days are mostly cleanup and learning your payers and workflows. Expect the clearest change in aged A/R and repeat denial reasons by the second and third months. No partner can honestly guarantee a specific result before seeing your data.

## Key takeaways

- Small practices feel denial, payment-rate and staffing pressure faster because they have less slack.
- Outsource RCM when billing depends on one person, A/R over 90 days keeps growing or the owner is doing claims work.
- Keep it in-house when an experienced team already keeps denials low and A/R current.
- Partial outsourcing, such as aged A/R or eligibility, is a low-risk way to start.
- Insist on work inside your own systems, monthly KPI reporting, a BAA and a clear exit path.
