Cardiology billing outsourcing makes sense when your claims are high value, your service mix spans office, facility and remote monitoring, and a small team can't give each claim the review it needs. Cardiology can't run on a volume-first billing process. One denied catheterization, ablation or imaging study can cost more than a week of clean office visits. The right partner adds pre-service checks and pre-submission review for the claims that matter most.

## Why cardiology billing needs its own process

Most billing workflows are built for volume: many low-value claims, submitted fast, with denials worked afterward. Cardiology has a different shape:

- **Mixed settings.** The same physician may bill office visits, diagnostic tests read in the office, and procedures in a hospital or ambulatory setting. Payment and coding rules differ by setting.
- **Testing and imaging.** Echocardiograms, stress tests, nuclear studies and cardiac CT often require authorization from commercial and Medicare Advantage plans.
- **Remote monitoring.** Device checks and remote patient monitoring require specific time, day-count and documentation thresholds.
- **High dollar value per claim.** A small error rate on procedures does more damage than the same error rate on visits.

That is why a cardiology practice should judge a billing partner on how it protects high-value claims, not just how many claims it submits.

## What changed in Medicare payment for cardiology

Medicare payment shifted in 2026 in ways that matter for cardiology billing.

CMS's [CY 2026 Physician Fee Schedule final rule](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/calendar-year-cy-2026-medicare-physician-fee-schedule-final-rule-cms-1832-f) raised the nonqualifying APM conversion factor to $33.40, up 3.26%, but also applied a -2.5% "efficiency adjustment" to work RVUs for non-time-based services. CMS estimated the overall effect on cardiology at about +1%, according to the [American College of Cardiology](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2025/10/31/21/33/cms-releases-2026).

The average hides a split. In its [detailed analysis](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Articles/2025/11/06/12/51/Dive-Into-the-2026-Medicare-Physician-Fee-Schedule-Final-Rule), the ACC noted that facility-based cardiology services were projected to decline 7% while office-based services were projected to increase 5%. Practices whose revenue leans on hospital procedures felt a very different year than office-heavy practices.

For 2027, CMS's [final rule](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/calendar-year-cy-2027-medicare-physician-fee-schedule-final-rule) sets the nonqualifying APM conversion factor at $32.63, a 2.31% decrease from 2026. Lower rates leave less room for denials, write-offs and underpayments.

## New codes add complexity

The [AMA's CPT 2026 code set](https://ama-assn.org/press-center/ama-press-releases/ama-releases-cpt-2026-code-set) includes 418 changes. Several touch cardiology directly:

- New remote monitoring codes for 2 to 15 days of data within a 30-day period, and management codes that start after 10 minutes per calendar month instead of 20.
- New codes for AI-based coronary atherosclerotic plaque assessment using coronary CT angiography data.
- Codes for algorithmic detection of cardiac dysfunction.

Every new code needs charge master updates, documentation templates and payer-by-payer checks on coverage. That work often falls through the cracks in a busy practice.

Remote monitoring deserves special care. The HHS Office of Inspector General [found](https://oig.hhs.gov/reports/all/2024/additional-oversight-of-remote-patient-monitoring-in-medicare-is-needed) that about 43% of Medicare enrollees who received remote patient monitoring did not receive all three components of it. Clean documentation is your best defense in an audit.

## Prior authorization is a revenue issue, not just a paperwork issue

In the [AMA's prior authorization survey](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/prior-authorization/exhausted-prior-auth-many-patients-abandon-care-ama-survey), 94% of physicians said prior authorization delays access to necessary care, and 78% said it often or sometimes leads patients to abandon treatment. Practices reported completing 43 prior authorizations per physician per week, using about 12 hours of physician and staff time.

For cardiology, an authorization that's missing or doesn't match the performed service is one of the most expensive denials you can get. Check authorization before the date of service and compare the authorized code with the performed code before you submit.

One note on Medicare: CMS's [WISeR model](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/innovation/innovation-models/wiser), which adds prior authorization in six states from 2026, doesn't include cardiac imaging or cardiac procedures among its selected services. Commercial and Medicare Advantage rules are still where most cardiology authorization work happens. ## A cardiology billing workflow that protects high-value claims

| Stage | What to do | Why it matters in cardiology |
|---|---|---|
| Scheduling | Verify eligibility and benefits when the test or procedure is booked | Prevents denials on high-cost services |
| Pre-service | Obtain and log authorization, including codes and units | Mismatched codes are a common denial reason |
| Documentation | Use templates that capture medical necessity and required elements | Supports audits on imaging and monitoring |
| Pre-submission | Review high-value claims against the authorization and notes | Catches errors before they become denials |
| Payment posting | Compare payments with expected reimbursement by code | Surfaces underpayments that look like normal payments |
| Denials and A/R | Work high-dollar denials first and track root causes | Recovers the most revenue per hour worked |

## What to ask a cardiology billing partner

- Which cardiac procedures, tests and monitoring services do your coders work on every week?
- How do you check authorizations against performed codes before submission?
- Do you review high-value claims manually before they go out?
- Can you report denials, A/R and collections by service line, such as procedures, imaging, monitoring and visits?
- How do you keep up with CPT and fee schedule changes each year?
- Will the same dedicated team work our account every day?

## When not to outsource

If your billing team already knows cardiology, catches authorization mismatches before submission, and keeps denials and aged A/R low, a full handoff may not add much. Consider outsourcing one piece, such as authorizations or aged A/R, instead.

In our work with a cardiovascular practice, better management of the scheduling and front-end process increased monthly billing by several thousand dollars without adding provider hours. Front-end discipline is often where cardiology revenue is won or lost.

## Key takeaways

- Cardiology claims are high value and span office, facility and monitoring services, so they need more review than a volume-first process provides.
- The 2026 Medicare rule helped office-based cardiology services and hurt facility-based ones, and the 2027 conversion factor is lower.
- New CPT codes for remote monitoring and AI-based cardiac analysis require careful setup and documentation.
- Verify authorizations before service and compare them with performed codes before submission.
- Choose a partner that reports by service line and gives high-value claims a manual review.
