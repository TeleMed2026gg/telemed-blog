An in-house medical biller costs far more than the salary on the offer letter. Once you add payroll taxes, benefits, software, training, recruiting and the work that stops when that person is out, the real number is well above base pay. Benefits alone add roughly 43 cents to every wage dollar, according to federal data. Outsourcing makes sense when that fully loaded cost, plus the risk of a single point of failure, is higher than a vendor's total fee for the same scope.

## Start with salary, then load it

The closest federal benchmark for billing and coding staff is the Bureau of Labor Statistics category for medical records specialists. BLS reports a [median annual wage of $51,140, or $24.59 per hour, in May 2025](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/medical-records-and-health-information-technicians.htm). Pay varies by market and by experience. Certified coders and billers who handle complex specialties earn more.

Salary is only part of what an employee costs. In the BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation series, [private-industry wages averaged $32.82 per hour and benefits $14.07 per hour in June 2026](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/compensation-costs-for-private-industry-workers-averaged-46-89-per-hour-worked-in-june-2026.htm). Benefits were 30% of total compensation. Put another way, every dollar of wages carried about 43 cents of benefits on top.

## A fully loaded cost worksheet

Use your own numbers. The example below is an **illustration** built on the BLS median and the national benefits ratio, not a quote or a benchmark for your market.

| Cost item | How to estimate it | Illustration (annual) |
|---|---|---|
| Base salary | Offer letter or local salary data | $51,140 |
| Benefits and payroll taxes | Your actual rate, or ~43% of wages nationally | ~$21,900 |
| Billing software, clearinghouse and seat licenses | Per-user fees from your vendors | Your number |
| Training and certification | Courses, CEUs, payer webinars | Your number |
| Recruiting and onboarding | Job ads, agency fees, manager time | Your number |
| Equipment and space | Workstation, phone, share of rent | Your number |
| Coverage gaps | Days the biller is out × claims not worked | Your number |
| **Fully loaded cost** | Sum of the above | **$73,000+ before overhead** |

Two lines on that table get skipped most often.

**Coverage gaps.** When a solo biller takes two weeks of vacation, claims don't submit, denials don't get worked and payments don't post. The cost shows up 30 to 60 days later as a dip in collections. If a claim ages past a payer's filing limit, it may never be paid.

**Turnover.** Every time a biller leaves, you pay to recruit, you lose weeks of productivity while the new person learns your payers and your EHR, and knowledge about open appeals often leaves with them.

## What you get from an in-house biller

Cost isn't the only factor. An in-house biller has real advantages:

- **Proximity.** They can walk over to a provider and fix a documentation gap the same day.
- **Context.** They know your patients, your payer mix and your quirks.
- **Control.** You set priorities daily without going through a vendor.

For a small practice with steady volume and a biller who has been there for years, that combination is hard to beat.

## What outsourcing changes

An outsourced team replaces a single person with a process. In practice, that usually means:

- **No single point of failure.** Someone else covers when an agent is out.
- **Broader scope.** The same team can handle charge entry, claim submission, payment posting, denial work, A/R follow-up and eligibility checks.
- **Reporting.** A good vendor reports clean claim rate, denial rate, days in A/R and collections every month.
- **Scalability.** Adding a provider or a location doesn't mean another hiring cycle.

It also introduces new costs and risks. You still need someone in-house who owns the relationship, reviews reports and escalates problems. You need clear access controls for your systems. And any vendor that touches protected health information should sign a Business Associate Agreement under HIPAA. ## How to compare the two honestly

Comparing a salary with a vendor's percentage-of-collections fee is apples to oranges. Put both on the same basis:

1. **Calculate your fully loaded in-house cost** using the worksheet above.
2. **List the work actually being done today.** Many in-house billers submit claims but don't have time for A/R over 90 days or denial appeals.
3. **Ask vendors to price the same scope.** Include the work that's falling through the cracks today, not just what your biller does now.
4. **Estimate the revenue difference.** If an outsourced team works aged A/R and appeals that nobody touches today, the recovered collections belong in the comparison.
5. **Run a pilot when possible.** A defined pilot on one function, measured in dollars collected, tells you more than any proposal.

## When to keep billing in-house

Outsourcing isn't the right answer for everyone. Keep billing in-house if:

- Your claim volume is low and stable, and one experienced biller keeps up without backlog.
- Your A/R over 90 days is small and shrinking.
- Your specialty has unusual payer rules your biller knows well and a vendor would need months to learn.
- You don't have anyone who can manage a vendor relationship.

Consider outsourcing, fully or in part, if you've lost a biller in the last two years, if aged A/R keeps growing, if denials go unworked, or if your office manager is filling in on billing.

## A hybrid option

Many practices keep one in-house lead who knows the providers and outsource the high-volume, repeatable work: eligibility verification, payment posting, A/R follow-up and denial rework. The in-house lead becomes the reviewer and escalation point. This keeps context close and removes the single point of failure.

## Key takeaways

- The median wage for medical records specialists was $51,140 in May 2025, according to BLS.
- Benefits averaged 30% of private-industry compensation in June 2026, roughly 43 cents per wage dollar.
- Add software, training, recruiting, equipment and coverage gaps to get the real number.
- Compare in-house and outsourced options on the same scope of work, including tasks nobody does today.
- Keep billing in-house when volume is low, staff is stable and A/R is under control.
